# Tango database audit: 10 questions resolved with community consensus

Your tango game database has several classifications correct, but this research reveals **three factual errors and two tier misplacements** that need immediate correction. The findings below draw from El Recodo discography data, Tango Voice's four-tier DJ system, the Weigel Buenos Aires milonga survey (14 milongas, 2008), DJ SuperSabino's 80-DJ international survey, todotango.com, and multiple tango DJ community sources.

---

## 1. Pugliese's 400+ recordings make 63 a workable but thin selection

Osvaldo Pugliese recorded **over 400 studio recordings** across a 42-year career (1943–1985) for Odeón, Stentor, and Philips. This figure comes from the Tangoteca/Lefcovich discography project and is consistent with El Recodo's multi-page database. His singers included Roberto Chanel, Alberto Morán (who recorded 50+ tracks alone with Pugliese), Jorge Vidal, Jorge Maciel, and Alfredo Belusi.

**63 playable songs is adequate but borderline for a Big 4 orchestra.** Tango DJs typically keep **20–40 Pugliese tracks** in active milonga rotation, meaning 63 covers the danceable core. However, many of Pugliese's later concert-style recordings (1960s onward) are less danceable, so the real question is selection quality. The essential Pugliese tracks that DJs cite repeatedly include La Yumba, Gallo Ciego, Emancipación, Nochero Soy, La Mariposa, Recuerdo, Mala Junta, Chiqué, Negracha, La Rayuela, La Tupungatina, Yunta de Oro, Zum, Boedo, Malandraca, and Pasional (w/ Morán). If your 63 tracks cover these and the best vocal recordings, the gap is manageable. Compared to D'Arienzo (who recorded roughly 800+ sides), 63 is proportionally thin for a Big 4 member, but not a critical deficiency if curated well.

## 2. La Yumba: confirmed iconic, recorded 1946, and yes — De Angelis covered it

**La Yumba is unambiguously Pugliese's most iconic composition.** Every authoritative source confirms this: todotango.com calls it "a sort of anthem of his orchestra," it was played at his funeral, and the "yumba" beat pattern became synonymous with Pugliese's entire style. The term "yumba" was coined by Pugliese himself.

Pugliese first recorded La Yumba on **August 21, 1946** (Odeón 7686, catalog #15758), per todotango.com and SecondHandSongs. He re-recorded it on November 13, 1952 (Odeón 55521 — though discographer Nicolás Lefcovich suspects this may be a "laboratory manipulation" of the 1946 master) and again on December 26, 1985, at his Teatro Colón 80th birthday concert.

**Alfredo De Angelis did record La Yumba** — as an instrumental on **April 19, 1974** (Odeón 6576-A, catalog #41349). This is confirmed on todotango.com's La Yumba song page and appears on Spotify under De Angelis's "Colección Aniversario" compilation. It is a post-Golden Age recording made 28 years after the original.

## 3. Biagi at Level 2 is correct — he's the strongest "Core" orchestra but not an Icon

**Level 2 (Core) is the right placement for Rodolfo Biagi.** The "Big 4" designation (D'Arienzo, Di Sarli, Troilo, Pugliese) is deeply entrenched across virtually every authoritative source — Paul Lohman, TejasTango, milonga.co.uk, TangoAndChaos.org. No mainstream tango source includes Biagi in this group.

However, Biagi is arguably the **strongest Level 2 orchestra**. In the SuperSabino DJ survey (80 international DJs), Pugliese, Biagi, and Fresedo were in a statistical dead heat for 5th place when DJs named their "must-play" orchestras. In the Weigel Buenos Aires survey, Biagi's play frequency was **not statistically distinguishable from Troilo's** (Fisher's test p = 0.423). Biagi ranked high across all three genres — tango, vals, and milonga — which is rare. Tango Voice places him in "Tier II: High Priority" alongside Tanturi, Caló, D'Agostino, Donato, and Rodriguez.

The case for promoting Biagi to Level 1 is tempting but ultimately unsupported by consensus. His historical role as D'Arienzo's pianist during the pivotal 1935–1938 period gives him enormous influence, and his rhythmic style makes him indispensable for DJ programming. But the Big 4 framework has cultural inertia that no data-driven argument has overturned.

## 4. Hector Varela should be demoted to Level 3

**Level 2 (Core) is too high for Varela.** Tango Voice explicitly notes it "would be very unusual to hear the music of Salgan, Sassone, and Varela in Buenos Aires milongas." Varela is absent from Tango Voice's four-tier system entirely. TejasTango classifies him as "Transition Era" alongside Sassone and Francini/Pontier — orchestras that created concert-style sound less suited to dancing.

Compared to genuine Level 2 orchestras, the gap is stark. **Canaro, Tanturi, and De Angelis** all appear in Tango Voice's main 12 Buenos Aires milonga orchestras; Varela does not. Experienced DJ Tomas Kohl writes candidly: "When I was a new DJ, I liked to play Varela a lot. I suppose that many times I was the only person in the room having fun." Milonga.co.uk notes that interest in Varela "only emerged around 2009" and he still lacks a foothold in traditional Buenos Aires milongas. His post-golden-age recordings (1950s+) and intense emotional style limit his accessibility. **Level 3 is the correct placement.**

## 5. Angel Vargas as a separate Level 3 entity is correct

**Listing "Angel Vargas" as a separate Level 3 orchestra is historically accurate.** After splitting from D'Agostino in 1946, Vargas functioned as a bandleader, recording **86 sides** with orchestras directed by Eduardo Del Piano, Armando Lacava, Edelmiro D'Amario, José Libertella, and Luis Stazo. El Recodo maintains a separate discography page for "Ángel Vargas as single performer."

The standard tango database convention is clear: the **93–94 recordings with D'Agostino (1940–1946)** should be categorized under D'Agostino's orchestra, with Vargas listed as vocalist. The solo recordings belong under "Ángel Vargas" as the lead artist. This dual listing is universal across todotango.com and El Recodo. The critical caveat is that Vargas's solo material is **far less commonly played at milongas** than the D'Agostino partnership recordings, making Level 3 the appropriate tier for his independent work.

## 6. Gallo Ciego was recorded in 1959, not 1948 — a date correction is needed

**The database date of 1948 is wrong.** El Recodo definitively records Pugliese's "Gallo Ciego" as **July 23, 1959** (Odeón label, instrumental). This date is corroborated by Escuela de Tango de Buenos Aires (Marcelo Solis) and multiple discography sources. The composition is by Agustín Bardi, with earlier recordings by Julio De Caro (1927) and Ricardo Tanturi (1938).

Despite the date error, the track's importance is unquestionable. **Gallo Ciego is firmly in regular milonga rotation worldwide** — it is one of Pugliese's 3–4 most played instrumentals. TejasTango's Stephen Brown specifically pairs it with La Yumba as an "equally strong" tanda anchor. Contemporary orchestra Tango Bardo chose just four Pugliese tracks for their 2023 album: Gallo Ciego, Emancipación, Pata Ancha, and La Yumba — confirming its top-tier status.

## 7. El Marne is a tango, not a milonga — and D'Arienzo owns the definitive recording

**Two key findings here.** First, "El Marne" was indeed composed by **Eduardo Arolas** ("El Tigre del Bandoneón") in **1919**, named after the WWI Battle of the Marne. This is confirmed by todotango.com, Wikipedia, and academic sources including the College Music Symposium.

Second, and critically: **El Marne is classified as a tango, not a milonga**, across every major database. Todotango.com lists it as "Tango," El Recodo categorizes every recording (D'Arienzo 1939, Troilo 1952, Troilo 1967, Varela 1967, Maderna 1947) as "STYLE: TANGO," and the original sheet music was published as "El Marne (Gran Tango de Salon)." The confusion likely arises because D'Arienzo's up-tempo treatment can sound milonga-like and the piece uses a "tango-milonga" bass rhythm common in guardia vieja compositions.

**D'Arienzo's 1939 recording** (May 4, 1939, with pianist Juan Polito) is the most famous and most played version at milongas today. Troilo recorded it in 1952 and again in 1967, but D'Arienzo's version is the standard DJ choice.

## 8. Sassone at Level 3 is the right call

**Level 3 is correct for Florindo Sassone.** Multiple authoritative sources converge on this. Tango Voice groups him with Varela and Salgán as orchestras that would be "very unusual to hear at Buenos Aires milongas." Milonga.co.uk states plainly that "Sassone does not really belong with the first rank of orchestras." TejasTango classifies him as "Transition Era," noting his music is "more suitable for listening in the concert hall than for dancing in the milongas."

Sassone's recordings are primarily from **1957–1974** — firmly post-golden-age. His lush, large-ensemble orchestrations (sometimes featuring 30+ musicians) produce a concert sound that, while beautiful, sits outside the golden-age dance aesthetic most DJs prioritize. DJ Tomas Kohl observes: "I don't hear Sassone that often on milongas, and I wonder why?" He recommends Sassone only where "a 1950s Di Sarli instrumental tanda would fit" — an occasional, niche slot.

## 9. Orquesta Típica Victor should be Level 3, not Level 2

**Level 2 (Core) is too high for OTV.** Tango Voice places OTV in **Tier IV (Low Priority)** alongside Firpo, Lomuto, Malerba, Gobbi, and De Caro — the lowest of four tiers. OTV does not appear in any DJ survey data among tracked orchestras. TejasTango classifies them as "Old Guard," noting that "poor fidelity limits their usefulness for social dancing."

OTV was a **studio-only orchestra** (active 1925–1944) that "never appeared in public, being created exclusively for recording purposes." While historically important as Victor Records' house band, most of their recordings predate the golden age, and sound quality is a persistent issue. They appear in Tango Voice's secondary list of orchestras "played with some degree of regularity" at Buenos Aires milongas, but well below the core 12. **Level 3 is the appropriate placement** — they offer variety for knowledgeable DJs but are not a milonga staple.

## 10. "Milonga de mis amores" is an essential milonga piece — confirmed

**Yes, Pedro Laurenz's "Milonga de mis amores" is firmly in the canon of essential milonga-rhythm recordings.** Composed by Laurenz with lyrics by José María Contursi in 1937, it was first recorded on July 14, 1937, with singer Héctor Farrel. Laurenz re-recorded an instrumental version on January 14, 1944, which is the version most prized for dancing.

The evidence of its essential status is strong. El Bandoneón Records named an entire CD compilation after it ("Pedro Laurenz — Milonga De Mis Amores," EBCD 082). The Cambridge Companion to Tango includes it in their curated web listening list. Tanda of the Week (December 2025) featured it as the centerpiece of a Laurenz milonga tanda at Helsinki Milonga Weekend. It ranks in the **upper tier of milonga recordings**, below the most universally played pieces (Canaro's "Milonga sentimental," D'Arienzo's "La puñalada" and "De pura cepa") but firmly among the regularly programmed selections. It is Laurenz's most iconic milonga and an anchor piece for any Laurenz milonga tanda.

## Conclusion: five corrections for the database

This audit identified five items requiring action:

- **Gallo Ciego date**: Change from 1948 to **1959** (July 23)
- **Hector Varela**: Demote from Level 2 to **Level 3** — he is post-golden-age and far less played than true Core orchestras
- **Orquesta Típica Victor**: Demote from Level 2 to **Level 3** — Old Guard fidelity issues and Tier IV placement by DJ authorities
- **El Marne genre**: Reclassify from milonga to **tango** — every major database confirms this
- **De Angelis / La Yumba**: Add this 1974 instrumental recording if not already present

The remaining classifications — Biagi at Level 2, Sassone at Level 3, Angel Vargas as separate Level 3, Pugliese's 63-track count as adequate, and "Milonga de mis amores" as essential — are all validated by community consensus.
