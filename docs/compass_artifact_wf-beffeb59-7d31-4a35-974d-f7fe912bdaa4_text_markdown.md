# Last original recording years for 54 Argentine tango orchestras

**Seven database entries contain reissue dates masquerading as original recording years, and 10 more artists are missing a decade or more of recordings.** Across the full 54-artist catalog, only 8 golden-age entries have correct max years. The most egregious errors—Francisco Lomuto listed at 2015 when his last original session was 1927, and Domingo Federico at 2021 for a 1946 instrumental—trace to German digital reissue label Viamas GmbH and Spanish aggregator Novoson SL flooding streaming platforms with compilation albums that override original dates. This audit also corrects a factual error in the source data: **Ricardo Malerba died in 1974, not 1983** as stated.

## 4 special cases resolved

**Fulvio Salamanca is definitively a Golden Age historical figure**, not a modern artist. Born 1921 in Santa Fe, he served as pianist for Juan D'Arienzo before forming his own orquesta típica. Spanish Wikipedia documents **107+ commercial recordings** across three labels: 36 on Odeón (1957–63), 11 on Philips (1961), and 60 on Music Hall (1964–69). His orchestra featured luminaries including Fernando Suárez Paz and Simón Bajour. The database having just 1 song is a catastrophic gap—he needs approximately **106 additional recordings** added.

**Osmar Maderna recorded until one month before his death.** His final session on **March 29, 1951** with vocalist Adolfo Rivas produced his last sides, just 30 days before his fatal plane crash at age 33. Tango.info documents 57 recordings spanning 1946–1951. The database is missing his greatest works: *Lluvia de estrellas* (1948), *Pequeña* (1949), and *Escalas en azul* (1950) were all recorded after the current DB cutoff of 1946.

**Ángel D'Agostino's 1963 endpoint is confirmed correct.** His final sessions on May 20, 1963 with vocalist Raúl Lavié ("Mi chiquita" and "Yo te canto Buenos Aires") concluded a 23-year, 143-recording career. After his legendary partnership with Ángel Vargas ended in 1946, he continued with singers Tino García, Rubén Cané, Roberto Alvar, and others on RCA Victor through the early 1960s.

**Aníbal Troilo recorded until mid-1971, not 1969.** His final studio album, *Te acordás... Polaco?* with Roberto Goyeneche, was recorded between April and June 1971 at RCA Victor. The Argentine publication *Caras y Caretas* confirms: "El disco se convirtió en la última grabación de Aníbal Troilo." He continued performing live until his death in 1975 but never returned to the studio. The database is also missing his 1970 recordings including "For Export vol. 3" and the collaboration with D'Arienzo, *A mí me llaman Juan Tango*.

## The biggest gaps are hiding in plain sight

The most alarming finding is not the reissue-date contamination—those are obvious once flagged—but rather **10 artists whose databases simply stop decades too early**, hiding enormous portions of their recorded output.

**Osvaldo Fresedo** holds the record for the largest gap: the DB stops at 1955, but he recorded continuously on Columbia/CBS until his **retirement in 1980** with vocalist Argentino Ledesma, a span of **60 years** from first recording to last. **Lucio Demare** is similarly underrepresented: the DB stops at 1945, yet his complete restoration project (Tango Time Travel) documents recordings through **1968**, including a final solo piano LP on the Disc Jockey label. **Ricardo Tanturi**, perhaps the most surprising correction, is shown ending at 1948 when tango.info reveals he recorded with vocalist Osvaldo Ribó through 1958, with Horacio Roca through 1959, and made final instrumentals circa **1966**—18 years beyond the DB max.

**Francisco Canaro**'s gap is equally dramatic. The man who made nearly **3,800 recordings** over 48 years (1916–1964) shows a DB max of just 1949. Tango.info pinpoints his last recording as *Danza gaucha* on **November 6, 1964**, barely a month before his death. Similarly, **Enrique Rodríguez** maintained an exclusive 34-year relationship with Odeón, recording over 350 tracks through approximately **1971**, yet the DB cuts off at 1956.

## Ricardo Malerba: a death-year correction needed

The source data lists Malerba's death as 1983. This is incorrect. Every authoritative source—El-Recodo, TodoTango, Wikipedia, UCSB's Discography of American Historical Recordings—confirms Ricardo Francisco Malerba was born **August 24, 1905** and died **June 29, 1974**. His complete orchestral output spans 1941–1957 on RCA Victor, as documented by the blog *El Tango y sus Invitados*. The 2017 DB date corresponds to a Spotify digital compilation "Noches de Montmartre (1941–1944)."

## Detailed notes for every artist requiring correction

**Alberto Castillo** (DB: 1960 → actual: ~1976): After his famous Odeón period as vocalist/star (not strictly a musical director—his orchestras were led by arrangers like Emilio Balcarce and Eduardo Rovira), Castillo recorded for **Microfón with Osvaldo Requena's orchestra in 1975–76**. The Celenca discography on tango.info lists 257+ recordings through 1976. His DB should extend at minimum to 1976.

**Alfredo de Angelis** (DB: 2014 → actual: ~1989): Recorded **486 sides on Odeón** from July 1943 to January 1977. After Odeón ceased operations, he moved to EMI, recording singles in 1980 and 1985, with a final studio album *Así Es* released in **1989**. El-Recodo only covers his discography through 1962, which may explain the gap in tango-specific databases.

**Enrique Rodríguez** (DB: 1956 → actual: ~1971): Spanish Wikipedia states explicitly that Odeón hired him as exclusive artist and "esta relación se mantuvo durante 34 años, realizando más de 350 registros." Starting in 1937, 34 years equals 1971—the year he died. He toured Peru in 1965 and rebranded as "Enrique Rodríguez y su orquesta de todos los ritmos" in 1966.

**Florindo Sassone** (DB: 1968 → actual: ~1980): After his RCA Victor and Odeón periods, Sassone rotated among Carmusic, País, Music Hall, Embassy, and Microfón from 1971 onward. RateYourMusic lists original LP releases in 1972, 1975 (*Ribera sud*), and **1980** (*Buenos Aires mi ciudad*). He toured Japan in 1972 and Colombia/Venezuela in 1975.

**Horacio Salgán** (DB: N/A → actual: 1963 orchestra, ~1998 all formats): His orquesta típica discography on El-Recodo runs through **1963**. After that, his recorded output shifted to the Duo Salgán-De Lío (from 1957), Quinteto Real (from 1960), and solo piano. He appeared in the 1998 Oscar-nominated film *Tango, no me dejes nunca* performing with El Nuevo Quinteto Real. He lived to 100.

**Julio De Caro** (DB: 1952 → actual: 1975): His regular orchestral recordings ended in the early 1950s and he retired from his orchestra in 1954. However, Wikipedia confirms he "returned to a recording studio only in 1975" for the album *Los 14 de Julio de Caro*, a collaboration. This single comeback album extends his discography by 23 years.

**Mario Melfi** (DB: 1940 → actual: 1962): Based primarily in Paris, Melfi recorded under the name "Mario Melfi et son orchestre argentin." The blog *El Tango y sus Invitados* documents his complete works as spanning **1933–1962**, including tracks from 1953, 1955, 1961, and final recordings in 1962 (*Alondra*, *Mar del Plata*, *Tango Symphonie*).

**Rafael Canaro** (DB: 1939 → actual: ~1945): Francisco's brother established himself in Paris and recorded extensively in France and Italy. A TangoCD listing organizes his output as "Rafael Canaro (1929–1945)," and a detailed discography blog documents chronological recordings through the 1940s.

**Rodolfo Biagi** (DB: 1961 → actual: ~1962, possibly 1965): Tango Time Travel documents 136 studio recordings through 1948 and mentions "54 recordings made after 1950" for a future release covering "the 1950–1962 era." Tango.info lists entries for 1962 and a possible 1965 recording of *Milonga con variación*.

## Modern artists with outdated databases

Several active contemporary ensembles have DB max years that are **5–9 years behind** current output. **El Cachivache Quinteto** is the most extreme case: the DB shows 2016, but this prolific Buenos Aires group has released **15+ studio albums** through 2025, averaging more than one album per year since 2020. They are currently preparing their 16th album for late 2025. **Pablo Valle Sexteto** (now also recording as "Siempre Tango") has been releasing singles throughout 2024–2025, far beyond the DB's 2017 cutoff. **Orquesta Típica Andariega** released *Santo Milonguero* in May 2025.

Notable status updates: **Sexteto Milonguero disbanded in July 2020** (leader Javier Di Ciriaco announced the end), and **Gotan Project has been dormant since 2010**. **Color Tango** continues after founder Roberto Álvarez's death on March 5, 2023, with *Sentido Único* serving as his final recorded legacy.

## Conclusion

This audit reveals a systematic problem beyond individual data errors. **Digital reissue labels—particularly Viamas GmbH and Novoson SL—are the primary contamination vector**, injecting 21st-century compilation dates into what should be historical recording metadata. The pattern is consistent: streaming platforms default to the most recent release date rather than the original recording date, and databases that ingest from these platforms inherit the error. The 7 confirmed reissue-date entries should be corrected immediately, as they distort any analysis of recording activity timelines.

The second systemic issue is **incomplete ingestion of post-golden-age recordings**. Artists like Fresedo (60-year career truncated at year 28), Demare (30-year career truncated at year 7), and Tanturi (29-year career truncated at year 11) suggest the database may have been initially built from a single source that only covered certain label catalogs or time periods. El-Recodo.com, tango.info, and the Tango Time Travel restoration projects provide the most reliable complete discographies and should serve as primary references for filling these gaps.
